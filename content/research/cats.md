# Verified claims: cats

---

## The five pillars

AAFP and ISFM's *Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines* organise a cat's
environment around five pillars. The guidelines are explicit that addressing
environmental needs is **essential, not optional**, for feline wellbeing, and
that this applies in any setting — home, clinic or shelter.

1. **A safe place.** Somewhere the cat can retreat and feel secure rather than
   exposed. Usually elevated, enclosed on several sides, and never accessible
   to a dog or a child.
2. **Multiple, separated key resources** — food, water, litter, scratching
   posts, resting places, play areas. Separation matters as much as number: a
   cat that must pass another cat to reach the litter tray has, functionally,
   no litter tray.
3. **Opportunity for play and predatory behaviour.**
4. **Positive, consistent, predictable human–cat social interaction**, on the
   cat's terms.
5. **An environment that respects the cat's sense of smell**, meaning no
   strong cleaners or scents on the cat's own marked surfaces, and no forced
   scent mixing between cats.

— Ellis, Rodan, Carney, Heath, Rochlitz, Shearburn, Sundahl & Westropp (2013),
*AAFP and ISFM Feline Environmental Needs Guidelines*, Journal of Feline
Medicine and Surgery, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23422366/ ;
AAFP resource page,
https://catvets.com/resource/aafp-isfm-environmental-needs-guidelines/

## Litter boxes — the numbers

All from the AAFP/ISFM house-soiling guidelines
(https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11148882/), which restate the
environmental-needs guidance:

- **Number.** "In multi-cat households, the historical rule of thumb is to
  have at least one more litter box than the total number of cats" (n+1).
  **Households with a single cat should have two boxes, in two separate
  locations.**
- **Size.** A rectangular box **1.5 times the cat's length from nose to base
  of tail**. In one study adult cats preferred a 34 × 15 inch (86 × 39 cm) box
  over a smaller one.
- **Litter.** Fine, sand-like, unscented, clumping, to a depth of **at least
  1.25 inches (3cm)**. Avoid liners, slotted grills and deodorising powders.
- **Cleaning.** Scoop at least once a day; wash the box every 1–4 weeks.
- **Placement.** Do not put food and water next to the box. Cats perceive
  boxes placed adjacent to one another as one large box — separate them, or
  set the openings at right angles or around a corner.
- **Behavioural marker.** Cats without house-soiling problems dig in the box
  before eliminating more than house-soiling cats do.

> **Editorial note.** The "two boxes for one cat" line is the part almost
> nobody knows, and it comes straight from the guidelines. Lead with it.

## Introducing cats

There is no single published protocol with a fixed timeline; the consistent
shape across veterinary and shelter sources is:

1. **Full separation with scent exchange.** The new cat has its own room with
   all five resources. Swap bedding, or rub a cloth on one cat's cheeks and
   leave it near the other's resting place, daily.
2. **Site swapping.** Let each cat explore the other's space while the other
   is elsewhere, so both scents spread through the house without a meeting.
3. **Feeding either side of a closed door**, moving bowls closer over
   successive meals only as both cats stay relaxed and eating.
4. **Visual access under a barrier** — a cracked door, a baby gate, a screen.
5. **Short supervised meetings**, 5–10 minutes, with escape routes and
   vertical space available, ending before either cat is uncomfortable.

Typical timeline **2–4 weeks**; shy cats can take months. On any hiss, freeze,
flattened ears or stiff tail, go back to the last step that was working.

— San Diego Humane Society, https://sdhumane.org/resources/introducing-cats/ ;
VCA, https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/introducing-new-adult-cats ;
Ohio State Indoor Pet Initiative,
https://indoorpet.osu.edu/cats/feline-life-stressors/new-pets

> **Editorial note.** Do not present the 2–4 weeks as a guarantee. The
> failure mode is an owner who forces a meeting on day 3 because "the article
> said two weeks" and then has a lasting conflict to undo.

## Play and predation

**The predatory sequence is search, stalk, pounce, manipulate, consume.** A
wand toy covers stalk and pounce; a puzzle feeder covers manipulate and
consume. Ending a play session with food closes the loop.
— Preventive Vet, *Cat Play Sessions Using the Prey Sequence*,
https://www.preventivevet.com/cats/prey-sequence-cat-play-sessions

**Daily object play cut predation by a quarter; better food cut it by a
third.** Cecchetti et al. ran 355 cats from 219 households over three months,
with a seven-week baseline of prey brought home. Results:

- High-meat, grain-free commercial food: predation down **~36%**
- Five minutes a day of object play with a fishing-rod toy and a mouse toy:
  predation down **~25%**
- A collar bell: **no significant change**
- A Birdsbesafe collar cover: **no change in total prey brought home**
- Feeding the entire dry ration through a puzzle feeder: predation **up 33%**

— Cecchetti, Crowley, Goodwin & McDonald (2021), *Provision of High Meat
Content Food and Object Play Reduce Predation of Wild Animals by Domestic
Cats Felis catus*, Current Biology 31: 1107–1111,
https://www.cell.com/current-biology/fulltext/S0960-9822(20)31896-0

> **Editorial note.** The puzzle-feeder result is the interesting one and must
> not be dropped: giving a cat its *whole* dry ration through a puzzle
> increased hunting, plausibly because the cat was under-fed in practice.
> Puzzle feeders are good enrichment; they are not a way to ration a cat.

**Food puzzles are used by a minority of owners and get abandoned.** In
Delgado, Bain & Buffington's survey of 3,192 cat owners, 30% offered food
puzzles and a further 18% had tried and stopped. Case reports document food
puzzles helping with weight loss, anxiety and urination outside the box.
— Delgado, Bain & Buffington (2020), *A survey of feeding practices and use of
food puzzles in owners of domestic cats*, JFMS,
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1098612X19838080 ;
Dantas, Delgado, Johnson & Buffington (2016), *Food puzzles for cats*,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11148901/

## Vaccination (cats)

**Core for all pet cats:** feline herpesvirus-1 (FHV-1), feline calicivirus
(FCV), feline panleukopenia (FPV), and rabies. FeLV is core for cats under one
year old. Rabies is recommended for every cat regardless of lifestyle,
including indoor-only cats.

**Kitten schedule:** FVRCP starting at 6–8 weeks, boosters every 3–4 weeks
until **at least 16 weeks** of age. Rabies as a single dose at 12–16 weeks,
a booster at one year, then per product label. FeLV requires two doses 3–4
weeks apart from 8 weeks, after a negative FeLV test.

— AAHA/AAFP, *2020 Feline Vaccination Guidelines*,
https://www.aaha.org/resources/2020-aahaaafp-feline-vaccination-guidelines/core-vaccines-for-pet-cats/ ;
schedule table,
https://www.aaha.org/wp-content/uploads/globalassets/02-guidelines/feline-vaccination-guidlines/feline-vaccine-table.pdf

## Vet visits

**Carrier training with positive reinforcement measurably reduces distress**
around veterinary visits. The carrier should live out in the house as
furniture, not appear from a cupboard on the morning of an appointment.
— VCA, *Reducing the Stress of Veterinary Visits for Cats*,
https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/reducing-the-stress-of-veterinary-visits-for-cats

**Covering the carrier with a towel** gives the cat somewhere to hide and
lowers stress; a towel the cat has slept on carries familiar scent.
— AAHA, https://www.aaha.org/resources/how-to-reduce-stress-with-your-cat-at-the-vet/

**Synthetic facial pheromone** applied to carrier or bedding needs roughly 15
minutes before the cat goes in, so the alcohol carrier can evaporate.
— AAHA and VCA, as above.

## Pain and ageing

**Degenerative joint disease in cats is common and badly under-recognised.**
Reported prevalences vary with method and population: one radiographic study
found appendicular DJD in 74.26% of 101 cats; another found radiographic signs
in 33.9% with clinical signs in 16.5%, mostly in cats 10 and older; reviews
commonly cite roughly 60% of all cats and over 90% of cats over 12.

Crucially, **no obvious pain was detected on palpation** in any of the cats
with appendicular osteoarthritis in one of those studies. Feline arthritis
shows up as changed behaviour — jumping less, hesitating before a leap,
sleeping somewhere new, missing the litter box edge, grooming less — not as a
limp.

— Frontiers in Veterinary Science radiographic study,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7141235/ ;
Enomoto, Lascelles & Gruen (2020), *Development of a checklist for the
detection of degenerative joint disease-associated pain in cats*, JFMS,
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1098612X20907424 ;
dvm360 review,
https://www.dvm360.com/view/osteoarthritis-cats-what-we-now-know-about-recognition-and-treatment

> **Editorial note.** Quote a range and say why it varies, rather than picking
> the most dramatic number. The behavioural-signs list is the actionable part.

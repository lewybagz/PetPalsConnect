## Poisons

**Volume.** The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center handled more than 451,000
calls about toxic substances, plants and poisons in 2024 — a ~4% rise on the
year before. The 24-hour hotline is **(888) 426-4435**.
— ASPCA, *The Official Top 10 Toxins of 2024*,
https://www.aspca.org/news/official-top-10-toxins-2024

**Chocolate** made up 13.6% of exposures in 2024. Theobromine thresholds in
dogs: mild signs (vomiting, diarrhoea, excessive thirst) around **20 mg/kg**;
cardiotoxic effects **40–50 mg/kg**; seizures at **≥60 mg/kg**. Reported oral
LD50 for theobromine and caffeine is 100–200 mg/kg, but severe signs and death
occur well below that. Theobromine content varies enormously by product:
cocoa powder ~737 mg/oz, baking chocolate ~376 mg/oz, dark chocolate ~130–138
mg/oz, milk chocolate far less. Dogs clear theobromine over roughly 17.5
hours against 2–3 hours in humans, which is why it accumulates.
— Merck Veterinary Manual, *Chocolate Toxicosis in Animals*,
https://www.merckvetmanual.com/toxicology/food-hazards/chocolate-toxicosis-in-animals ;
ASPCA, as above.

**Xylitol** (in sugar-free gum, some peanut butters, baked goods, and some
medicines) is a leading component of the food-and-drink exposure category,
which ranked second overall in 2024.
— ASPCA, as above.

**Grapes and raisins** can cause gastrointestinal upset, lethargy, excessive
thirst, tremors, abdominal pain and, in severe cases, irreversible kidney
damage. No safe dose is established.
— ASPCA, as above.

**Lilies and cats: the highest-stakes plant in the house.** All species of
*Lilium* (true lilies) and *Hemerocallis* (daylilies) are lethally toxic to
cats. Every part is dangerous — flower, leaf, stem, pollen — and documented
cases include kidney injury from pollen alone, from licking pollen off the
coat, and from drinking vase water. GI signs begin 2–12 hours after ingestion;
kidney effects 12–96 hours. Treatment is early aggressive IV fluids and
typically 48–72 hours of hospitalisation.
— UC Davis Veterinary Medicine,
https://healthtopics.vetmed.ucdavis.edu/health-topics/feline/lily-toxicity-cats ;
FDA, *Lovely Lilies and Curious Cats*,
https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-health-literacy/lovely-lilies-and-curious-cats-dangerous-combination ;
International Cat Care, https://icatcare.org/articles/lily-poisoning-in-cats

> **Editorial note.** "Do not have lilies in a house with a cat" is one of the
> few pieces of pet advice that is genuinely absolute. Say it that way.

## Identification

**Microchips roughly double or vastly exceed unchipped return rates, and only
when the registration is current.** Lord et al. examined 7,704 microchipped
animals entering 53 US shelters between August 2007 and March 2008:

| | Not microchipped | Microchipped |
|---|---|---|
| Dogs returned to owner | 21.9% | **52.2%** |
| Cats returned to owner | 1.8% | **38.5%** |

Where a microchipped animal was *not* returned, the most common reason was
incorrect or missing owner information in the registry.

— Lord, Ingwersen, Gray & Wintz (2009), *Characterization of animals with
microchips entering animal shelters*, JAVMA 235(2): 160–167,
https://avmajournals.avma.org/view/journals/javma/235/2/javma.235.2.160.xml

**A chip is not a GPS tracker** and does nothing on its own — it is a number
that a scanner reads and a database resolves. The AAHA Universal Pet Microchip
Lookup Tool searches participating registries.
— AAHA, https://www.aaha.org/faqs/

## Body condition

**2022 APOP survey:** **59% of dogs and 61% of cats** were classified by
veterinary professionals as overweight or having obesity.

**The perception gap:** only **39% of dog owners and 45% of cat owners**
considered their own pet overweight. Only 46% of owners said their vet
discusses ideal weight annually; 12% said it never comes up.

— Association for Pet Obesity Prevention, 2022 survey,
https://www.petobesityprevention.org/2022 ; 2022 State of Pet Obesity Report,
https://www.petobesityprevention.org/2022-state-of-pet-obesity-report-summary

**Body condition scoring is hands-on, not visual**, and long or thick coats
and a cat's primordial pouch both make eyeballing unreliable. The standard
scales are 9-point (WSAVA/AAHA); 4–5 of 9 is ideal.
— APOP, as above.

## Choosing a professional

Dog training is **unregulated** in the US: anyone may use the title "trainer"
or "behaviourist". Independent certifications — issued by bodies that do not
also sell the training courses — are the meaningful signal.

- **CPDT-KA** (Certification Council for Professional Dog Trainers): ≥300
  hours of training experience in the past 3 years, an exam, and adherence to
  a code of ethics and a LIMA (least intrusive, minimally aversive) policy.
- **CPDT-KSA**: adds assessed video of real training sessions.
- **CBCC-KA** (CCPDT): behaviour consulting; ≥300 hours of behaviour
  consulting in 3 years plus a 200-question exam.
- **CDBC / CCBC** (International Association of Animal Behavior Consultants):
  advanced behaviour consulting for aggression, severe anxiety, phobias.
- **KPA CTP** (Karen Pryor Academy): a five-month program with written and
  practical assessment.
- **DACVB** (Diplomate, American College of Veterinary Behaviorists): a
  licensed veterinarian who completed a behaviour residency and board exam.
  The only one of these who can diagnose medical causes and prescribe.

— CCPDT/IAABC/KPA/ACVB summaries via
https://cattledogpublishing.com/blog/animal-trainers-and-behaviorists-licensing-and-certification/ and
https://baypathhumane.org/what-does-that-dog-training-certification-mean/

**Screening question that works:** ask what happens when the dog gets it
wrong. Per AVSAB, the answer should involve changing the setup or the
reinforcement, not applying something the dog wants to avoid.
— AVSAB Humane Dog Training Position Statement,
https://avsab.org/resources/position-statements/

## Adoption and settling in

**The "3-3-3 rule" (3 days to decompress, 3 weeks to learn the routine, 3
months to feel at home) has no research behind it.** **[weak]** It originates
in rescue practice, not study.

**What the data suggests instead:** in a 2025 adopter study only **30%** felt
their dog had settled within three months, and **41%** said adjustment took
four to six months. Reported estimates for full decompression commonly run
from six weeks to three months and beyond, and some behaviour can *worsen*
around month three as a dog relaxes enough to express itself.

— Discussion of the evidence and the 2025 adopter findings:
https://rescuedbytraining.com/2024/09/23/debunking-the-3-3-3-rule/ ;
https://www.thefarmersdog.com/digest/decompression-help-your-new-shelter-dog-feel-at-home/

> **Editorial note.** The rule's *shape* — expect a slow, non-linear settling,
> do not judge the dog by week one — is sound and humane. Its precision is
> invented. Write it as "the timeline is made up, the patience is not."

## Access: pet-friendly places

**Service animals under the ADA are dogs (and in limited cases miniature
horses) individually trained to perform tasks for a person with a
disability.** They must be allowed anywhere customers are permitted, including
dining rooms, bars, waiting areas and outdoor patios. No extra fee, no
segregation to a particular section.
— ADA.gov, *Service Animals*, https://www.ada.gov/topics/service-animals/

**Emotional support animals and therapy animals are not service animals under
the ADA** and carry no public-access right to restaurants and shops.
— ADA.gov, as above.

**A pet dog on a restaurant patio is at the business's discretion**, within
state and local health codes — some states permit dogs in outdoor seating
while barring them indoors, others delegate to local health departments.
Health codes never override ADA rights for service animals.
— WebstaurantStore summary of state rules,
https://www.webstaurantstore.com/blog/3812/service-animals.html ;
ADA.gov, as above.

> **Editorial note.** This matters to PetPalsConnect specifically: the seeded
> Location catalogue includes pet-friendly restaurants and cabins. "Listed as
> pet-friendly" is not "your dog may go inside", and the article should say
> to call ahead.

## Ticks

**Removal:** check the whole animal and remove attached ticks as soon as
possible, using tweezers or a purpose-made tick removal tool, wearing gloves.
— CAPC / Pets & Parasites,
https://www.petsandparasites.org/

**Prevention is exposure limitation plus year-round acaricide plus routine
checks**, and for some tick-borne diseases there is no vaccine — ehrlichiosis
among them — so screening matters.
— CAPC, as above.

**Geography, from CAPC's 2024 forecasts:**

- *Anaplasma*: principal risk in the Northeast and upper Midwest, spreading
  south and west, with forecast increases in eastern Kentucky, eastern
  Tennessee and north-western North Carolina.
  https://www.petsandparasites.org/expert-insights/anaplasmosis-forecast-2024/
- *Ehrlichia*: geographically patchy, with forecast prevalence higher than
  normal through much of the southern US.
  https://www.petsandparasites.org/expert-insights/ehrlichia-spp-forecast-2024/

**Anaplasmosis and Lyme share a vector**, so co-infection is possible.
— CAPC, as above.

**CAPC publishes county-level prevalence maps** of dogs testing positive,
which is the right thing to point a reader at rather than a national average.
— https://capcvet.org/

## Fleas

**The animal is the smallest part of the problem.** Adults live on the host;
eggs, larvae and pupae live in the environment. The overwhelming majority of a
flea population at any moment is off the pet — in carpet, bedding, floorboard
gaps and soil.
— Cornell Riney Canine Health Center, *Fleas*,
https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/fleas

**Numbers:** a female lays roughly **40 eggs per day**. Eggs hatch in **2–10
days**. The larval stage runs **5–20 days** depending on temperature and
humidity.
— Veterinary Prescriber, *The life cycle of a flea explained*,
https://www.veterinaryprescriber.org/free-articles/the-life-cycle-of-a-flea-explained ;
Cornell, as above.

**Pupae are the reason it takes months.** The pupal stage is resistant — it
cannot be reliably killed by insecticide, freezing or drying, and pupae can
remain dormant for months, emerging when triggered.
— Veterinary Prescriber, as above.

**Therefore:** treat every animal in the household, not just the itchy one, and
keep treating for **at least three months** — commonly quoted as the time
needed to clear an established infestation as dormant pupae emerge into
treatment.
— Cornell, as above; Itch, https://www.itchpet.com/blog/post/how-long-does-it-take-to-break-the-flea-life-cycle

## Leptospirosis

**Now core for all dogs.** The 2023 ACVIM consensus statement and the 2024
update to AAHA's guidelines recommend annual vaccination for **all dogs from 12
weeks of age, regardless of breed, geography or lifestyle**.
— Sykes et al. (2023), *Updated ACVIM consensus statement on leptospirosis in
dogs*, Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine,
https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jvim.16903 ;
AAHA NEWStat,
https://www.aaha.org/newstat/publications/leptospirosis-vaccination-recommended-to-be-core-for-most-dogs/

**It is not a rural disease.** The consensus statement notes prevalence in
small-breed dogs from urban areas, in puppies as young as 11 weeks, in
geriatric dogs, and in dogs inadequately vaccinated — including dogs given
2-serovar vaccines in some regions.
— Sykes et al., as above.

**The reservoir is rodents**, principally *Rattus norvegicus*, in which a
worldwide infection prevalence of around **30%** has been identified, exceeding
**80%** in some regions.
— Sykes et al., as above.

**It is zoonotic**, which is part of the stated rationale for making it core:
it can be severe or fatal despite treatment, and exposure is not predictable
from lifestyle.
— Sykes et al.; AVMA coverage,
https://www.avma.org/news/updated-guidance-canine-leptospirosis-reflects-better-understanding-disease

## Dental disease

**Prevalence is the headline.** Periodontal disease affects roughly **80% of
dogs and 70% of cats by age three**; figures of 80–89% in dogs over three are
reported.
— Cornell Riney Canine Health Center, *Periodontal disease*,
https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/health-topics/canine-health-information/periodontal-disease ;
*Overview of Periodontal Disease in Dogs and Cats*,
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41712493/

**"Dog breath" is not normal**, and the AVMA has campaigned on exactly that
point.
— AVMA, https://www.avma.org/news/press-releases/avma-doggie-breath-could-be-sign-serious-disease

**The Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) seal** identifies home-care
products that have met defined standards with supporting research — the
practical filter for a shelf full of claims.
— VOHC, https://vohc.org/

## Heartworm: the economics

**Prevention runs roughly $5–$25 a month** (about $60–$300 a year).

**Treatment:** melarsomine injections commonly quoted at **$400–$1,000**, with
total treatment typically **$600–$1,200** and severe cases running far higher.
Doxycycline and steroids add to it.

**And it costs the dog months.** Strict exercise restriction — crate rest with
brief lead walks only — runs **8–12 weeks**, continuing for at least a month
after the final injection.

— Vetster, *The cost of heartworm prevention vs. treatment in dogs*,
https://vetster.com/en/wellness/the-cost-of-heartworm-prevention-vs-treatment-in-dogs ;
PetMD, https://www.petmd.com/dog/parasites/how-much-does-heartworm-treatment-cost-dogs

> **Editorial note.** Quote cost ranges as ranges and date them; they vary by
> region and drift. The exercise-restriction figure is the one that lands
> hardest with owners of active dogs, and it is not a price — it is three
> months of a dog's life.

## When it is an emergency

Not a triage tool and not a diagnosis — a list of things that mean *go now*.

- **A cat straining to urinate and producing nothing**, especially a male.
  Urethral obstruction can be fatal within 24–48 hours.
- **A distended abdomen with unproductive retching** in a large, deep-chested
  dog. See GDV in `dogs.md`.
- **Difficulty breathing**: laboured or noisy breathing, gasping, or gums that
  are blue, grey or very pale. Open-mouth breathing in a cat is always
  significant.
- **Collapse, fainting, inability to stand, or severe disorientation.**
- **Seizures**, particularly repeated or lasting more than a few minutes.
- **Known toxin ingestion** — do not wait for signs.
- **Suspected heatstroke.** Cool first, transport second (see `dogs.md`).
- **Trauma**, including any hit by a vehicle, even if the animal seems fine.
- **Bleeding that does not stop with pressure.**
- **A pregnant animal straining without producing a neonate.**
- **Sudden hind-limb paralysis in a cat**, often with pain and cold feet.

— Composite of emergency-practice guidance:
https://evcc.com/blog/when-to-take-dog-cat-to-emergency-vet/ ;
https://pcvh.vet/signs-your-pet-needs-emergency-care/ ;
PetMD, *Urinary Tract Blockage in Cats*,
https://www.petmd.com/cat/conditions/urinary/urinary-tract-blockage-cats

## Being ready before it happens

**A first aid kit is a bridge to veterinary care, never a substitute.**
— AVMA, *Pets and disasters*,
https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/emergency-care/pets-and-disasters

**Contents commonly recommended:** gauze pads and rolls, self-adhesive bandage,
adhesive tape, blunt scissors, tweezers, digital thermometer, saline eye wash,
disposable gloves, a towel or blanket, a muzzle appropriate to the species (a
frightened animal in pain may bite), and copies of medical and vaccination
records — all in a waterproof container.
— American Red Cross, *Learn First Aid for Your Pets*,
https://www.redcross.org/content/dam/redcross/atg/PDF_s/Preparedness___Disaster_Recovery/Disaster_Preparedness/Pet_Safety/PetSafety.pdf ;
AVMA, as above.

**Evacuation kit adds:** several days of food and water in airtight containers,
medications, a spare leash, collar and ID, litter and a tray, carriers sized to
the animal, temporary ID tags, and current photographs of you with the animal
in case of separation.
— Red Cross, *Pet Disaster Preparedness*,
https://www.redcross.org/get-help/how-to-prepare-for-emergencies/pet-disaster-preparedness.html ;
Ready.gov, https://www.ready.gov/pets

**Numbers to store before you need them:** your vet, the nearest 24-hour
emergency hospital, ASPCA Animal Poison Control **(888) 426-4435**, and Pet
Poison Helpline **(855) 764-7661**.

## Getting there: cars

**There is no federal crash-test standard for pet travel harnesses.** The
Center for Pet Safety runs an independent certification programme, testing
against the federal standards used for child safety seats, including dynamic
crash testing at around 30mph. Certification is voluntary.
— Center for Pet Safety, *2013 Harness Study Results*,
https://centerforpetsafety.org/2013-harness-crash-test-study-results/ ;
Consumer Reports, https://www.consumerreports.org/car-safety/keep-pets-safe-in-the-car

**An unrestrained dog is a projectile**, to itself and to the people in the
car. An ordinary walking harness is not a restraint and is not built for those
loads.
— as above.

> **Editorial note.** Force figures for a given weight and speed circulate
> widely and vary with the assumptions behind them; describe the mechanism
> rather than quoting a specific poundage. The checkable facts are that the
> standard does not exist, that CPS certification is voluntary, and that a
> walking harness is not a crash restraint.

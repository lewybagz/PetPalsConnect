# Verified claims: dogs

Each entry is a claim, the number, and the source. Claims marked **[weak]**
are widely repeated but poorly evidenced — they appear in articles only with
that caveat attached.

## Socialisation and the sensitive period

**The primary socialisation window closes early.** AVSAB puts the first three
months as the period when sociability outweighs fear, and calls it the primary
window for adapting to new people, animals and experiences.
— AVSAB, *Position Statement on Puppy Socialization*.
https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Puppy_Socialization_Position_Statement_Download_-_10-3-14.pdf

**Socialisation should begin before the vaccine series finishes.** AVSAB's
position is that puppies can start classes as early as 7–8 weeks, after one
set of vaccines and a deworming, provided they show no signs of illness and
stay current on boosters.
— AVSAB, as above; summarised in dvm360,
https://www.dvm360.com/view/avsab-position-puppy-socialization-should-start-vaccinations

**The risk is asymmetric.** AVSAB states that more deaths in dogs under three
are attributable to behavioural problems than to infectious disease, and that
incomplete socialisation raises the risk of fear, avoidance and aggression
later.
— AVSAB, as above.

> **Editorial note.** This is the single most important framing in the dog
> corpus and the one owners get wrong in both directions: some isolate a puppy
> for sixteen weeks and lose the window, others take an unvaccinated puppy to
> a public dog park. The correct answer — controlled exposure, known dogs,
> clean surfaces, no public parks yet — is neither.

## Reading play

**The play bow is a punctuation mark, not just an opener.** A confident dog
uses it to start an interaction and to mark a pause mid-play. In dog and wolf
puppies, play bows followed moments of self-handicapping — where the stronger
partner briefly ceded the advantage.
— Byosiere et al., *Investigating the Function of Play Bows in Dog and Wolf
Puppies*, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5199004/

**Self-handicapping is a signal of healthy play.** The larger or stronger dog
deliberately making itself vulnerable — rolling, letting itself be caught,
mouthing softly — is what keeps asymmetric play mutual.
— Animal Humane Society, *Safe group play for dogs and puppies*,
https://www.animalhumanesociety.org/resource/safe-group-play-dogs-and-puppies

**The consent test.** Hold one dog back gently for a few seconds and release.
If the other dog re-invites — play bow, bounce, circling back — and the
restrained dog returns eagerly, the play is mutual. If only one dog wants to
continue, it was not play for the other one.
— Animal Humane Society, as above.

**Healthy play has structure:** bouncy, exaggerated, loose movement; frequent
role reversals and activity shifts; soft mouths; natural pauses where both
dogs reset. Continuous, silent, one-directional pursuit with no pauses is the
pattern to interrupt.
— Animal Humane Society, as above.

## Play styles

Breed group is a loose predictor of *style*, not of temperament:

- **Herding breeds** (Border Collie, Australian Shepherd, German Shepherd)
  favour chase, circling and controlling another dog's movement.
- **Terriers**, especially the bull breeds, favour wrestling and body contact.
- **Retrievers and other sporting breeds** favour body-slamming, neck-biting
  and roughhousing.

Mismatches are the common source of trouble: not every dog that likes to run
enjoys being chased, and a chaser can read as a threat to a dog that does not
play that way.
— Elanco, *What's Your Dog's Play Style?*,
https://yourpetandyou.elanco.com/us/lifestyle/what-is-your-dogs-play-style ;
Patricia McConnell, *Play Styles & Status Seeking*,
https://www.patriciamcconnell.com/theotherendoftheleash/play-styles-status-seeking-correlated/

**Tug of war does not make a dog "dominant".** Rooney & Bradshaw ran 14 Golden
Retrievers through 20 sessions of tug they were allowed to win and 20 they
lost. Dominance dimensions of the dog–human relationship were unaffected by
who won. The authors did note that outcomes may differ when play signals are
absent or misread — the game needs to look like a game.
— Rooney & Bradshaw (2002), *An experimental study of the effects of play upon
the dog–human relationship*, Applied Animal Behaviour Science,
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159101001927

## Introductions

**Neutral ground, parallel first.** Dogs meet in a space neither has claimed,
walking in the same direction with roughly 10–20 feet (3–6m) between them,
closing 3–5 feet at a time as both stay loose. Start far enough apart —
ideally 30 feet (9m) — that neither dog is over threshold.
— Animal Humane Society, *How to successfully introduce two dogs*,
https://www.animalhumanesociety.org/resource/how-successfully-introduce-two-dogs ;
Mid Atlantic Lab Rescue, *Dog–Dog Introductions: Parallel Walking*,
https://www.mlar.org/media/1630/5-dog-dog-introductions.pdf

**Head-on, on-leash, through a fence is the worst version of a greeting** —
the leash prevents the arc dogs naturally walk, and the barrier adds
frustration.
— Animal Humane Society, as above.

**Dogs and cats in one household** need a gradual protocol: separation and
scent swapping first, then feeding either side of a closed door, then visual
access through a baby gate in short 5–10 minute sessions, then supervised
meetings with the dog leashed. The cat needs vertical escape routes in every
shared room; a cat that cannot leave will defend itself.
— San Diego Humane Society, *Introducing Dogs and Cats*,
https://sdhumane.org/resources/introducing-dogs-and-cats-complete/ ;
Ohio State Indoor Pet Initiative,
https://indoorpet.osu.edu/dogs/new_additions_dogs/introducing-dogs-and-cats ;
International Cat Care, https://icatcare.org/articles/introducing-cats-and-dogs

## Dog parks

**Roughly one in five fresh faecal samples at US dog parks carried intestinal
parasite eggs.** A survey across 30 US cities found ~20% of fresh samples
positive for gastrointestinal helminth eggs — hookworms most common in the
South and Midwest, roundworm in Western cities. Park entrances are the most
contaminated spot in a park.
— Nationwide dog-park survey, aligned with Companion Animal Parasite Council
data; see https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405939025000887
and https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11949369/

**Regional prevalence in dogs:** roughly 1 in 10 dogs in the US Northeast
carries roundworms, hookworms or whipworms; approaching 1 in 5 in the Midwest;
between 1 in 5 and 1 in 2 in the South.
— CAPC-aligned prevalence data, as above.

**Public dog parks are also where respiratory disease spreads** — kennel
cough, canine influenza, leptospirosis — and where fights happen. Reported
owner surveys put attacks in public dog parks at more than one in seven
owners, with most incidents occurring when owners were not watching.
— Elanco, https://yourpetandyou.elanco.com/us/lifestyle/are-dog-parks-safe ;
public-health review, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5830495/

> **Editorial note.** The "1 in 7" figure comes from an owner survey reported
> secondhand and should be described as a survey finding, not an incidence
> rate. The parasite figures are the stronger ones; lead with those.

**Dogs that should not be at a public dog park:** puppies before the vaccine
series is complete, immunocompromised dogs, dogs that guard resources (water
bowls, toys, their person), dogs recovering from injury, and dogs in season.
— Elanco, as above; VCA, *Proper Etiquette at the Dog Park*,
https://vcahospitals.com/know-your-pet/proper-etiquette-at-the-dog-park

**Etiquette that reduces incidents:** leash off before entering the play area
but on through the airlock; move away from the gate immediately; bring one or
two dogs at most so you can actually watch them; leave high-value toys and
food at home, as they raise the odds of conflict; put the phone away;
intervene early on humping, guarding or bullying.
— VCA, as above; Best Friends Animal Society,
https://bestfriends.org/pet-care-resources/dog-park-etiquette-rules-faqs

## Heat

**Ground temperature is not air temperature.** Reported figures: at 86°F
(30°C) air, asphalt reaches about 135°F (57°C); at 87°F (31°C), about 143°F
(62°C). Pavement around 125°F (52°C) — reached at roughly 77°F (25°C) air —
can burn a paw pad in about 60 seconds.
— Four Paws International,
https://www.four-paws.org/our-stories/publications-guides/hot-asphalt-a-danger-to-your-dogs-paws ;
AKC, https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/dog-paws-hot-pavement/

**The seven-second test:** press the back of your hand to the surface. If you
cannot hold it there for seven seconds, your dog should not walk on it.
— Four Paws International, as above.

**Flat-faced dogs are at markedly higher risk.** A 2024 UK emergency-practice
study put brachycephalic dogs at 4.21 times the odds of heat-related illness
versus mesocephalic dogs (95% CI 3.22–5.49, p<0.001). Hall et al. (2020),
across 905,543 dogs, found brachycephalic dogs had greater odds of all three
categories of heat-related illness.
— Beard et al. (2024), *Epidemiology of heat-related illness in dogs under UK
emergency veterinary care in 2022*, Veterinary Record,
https://bvajournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/vetr.4153 ;
Hall et al. (2020), summarised at
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13011106/

**Exertional heatstroke is the bigger killer, not hot cars.** UK data found
exercise-related heat illness far outnumbered vehicle-related cases.
— Hall et al., https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7459873/

**Cooling: cold water is correct, and "tepid only" is a myth.** Cold-water
immersion (conductive) and water spray with air movement (evaporative) are the
recommended methods. Cold-water immersion suits young, healthy dogs; the
evaporative method suits older dogs or those with underlying disease. Use
whatever water is available provided it is cooler than the dog. The 2016
prehospital guidance is **"cool first, transport second"**.
— Cooling methods study,
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10385239/ ; RVC fact file,
https://www.rvc.ac.uk/small-animal-vet/teaching-and-research/fact-files/heatstroke-in-dogs-and-cats

> **Editorial note.** "Never use cold water, it causes shock" is one of the
> most persistent and most dangerous pieces of pet folklore. It is worth
> correcting explicitly rather than just stating the right advice. Avoid
> packing a dog in ice — that constricts skin blood flow and slows heat loss —
> but do not withhold cold water.

## Water

**Blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) can kill within hours.** Blooms may look
green, blue, yellow, red or brown, often like spilled paint or pea soup, and
toxicity cannot be judged by sight. Microcystins damage the liver, with death
in 12–24 hours to a few days; anatoxins attack the nervous system and can kill
within minutes to hours. There is no antidote; treatment is supportive.
— Cornell Riney Canine Health Center,
https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/riney-canine-health-center/canine-health-topics/blue-green-algae-poisoning-cyanobacteria-toxicosis ;
CDC, https://www.cdc.gov/harmful-algal-blooms/hcp/veterinarians/index.html

**Water intoxication (hyponatremia) is a real risk for hard-swimming dogs.**
Repeated diving and retrieving, or biting at a hose or sprinkler, can flood
sodium levels. Early signs: pale gums, lethargy, disorientation, stumbling —
which can progress within minutes to laboured breathing, collapse and
seizures. Small dogs and high-drive dogs are most at risk.
— AKC, https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/health/can-dogs-drink-much-water/ ;
Veterinary Poisons Information Service,
https://www.vpisglobal.com/2019/08/01/water-intoxication/

## Puppies and growth

**The "five minutes per month of age" rule has no research behind it.**
**[weak]** It is widely repeated and cannot be traced to a study.
— https://www.vetvoices.co.uk/post/puppy-exercise-5-minuets-per-month-of-life

**What was actually measured:** Krontveit et al. followed more than 500
Newfoundlands, Labradors, Leonbergers and Irish Wolfhounds in Norway.
Climbing stairs in the first three months was associated with higher risk of
radiographic hip dysplasia. Daily off-leash exercise in park-like, moderately
rough terrain, and being born on a farm, were associated with *lower* risk.
The protective factor was not less exercise — it was free, varied, self-paced
exercise on forgiving ground.
— Krontveit et al. (2012), American Journal of Veterinary Research 73(6):
838–846; discussed at
https://www.veterinaryirelandjournal.com/small-animal/392-guidelines-for-exercising-pups-separating-myths-from-science

## Enrichment

**Two weeks of nosework made dogs measurably more optimistic.** Duranton &
Horowitz gave 10 dogs daily nosework and 10 dogs heelwork for two weeks, with
a cognitive bias (optimism) test before and after. Only the nosework group
reduced their latency to approach an ambiguous stimulus — the standard read-out
for optimism in animals.
— Duranton & Horowitz (2019), *Let me sniff! Nosework induces positive
judgment bias in pet dogs*, Applied Animal Behaviour Science,
https://psychology.barnard.edu/sites/default/files/inline-files/Let%20me%20sniff.pdf

> **Editorial note.** n=20 across two groups. Report it as a small study with
> a clear result, not as settled fact. It is still the best available evidence
> for something owners can do in a back garden for free, and it maps directly
> onto the app's `sniffari` activity.

## Vaccination (dogs)

**Core vaccines** — recommended for all dogs regardless of lifestyle unless
there is a specific medical reason not to: canine distemper virus, canine
adenovirus type 2, canine parvovirus type 2, **leptospirosis**, and rabies.
— AAHA, *2022 Canine Vaccination Guidelines* and the 2024 update,
https://www.aaha.org/resources/2022-aaha-canine-vaccination-guidelines/recommendations-for-core-and-noncore-canine-vaccines/

**Leptospirosis moved from noncore to core** in the 2024 update, following
ACVIM and WSAVA consensus statements — driven by rising prevalence, improved
vaccine safety, and how widespread the bacteria are environmentally. ACVIM
recommends annual administration to all dogs from 12 weeks of age regardless
of breed.
— AAHA NEWStat,
https://www.aaha.org/newstat/publications/leptospirosis-vaccination-recommended-to-be-core-for-most-dogs/

**Rabies:** a single dose at 12–16 weeks of age in accordance with local law,
then a booster one year later regardless of formulation or age at first dose.
— AAHA, as above.

**The 16-week line matters.** The puppy series runs to at least 16 weeks
because maternal antibody can block earlier doses; a puppy that finished at 12
weeks is not finished.
— AAHA, as above.

**Annual heartworm testing plus year-round prevention.** AHS and the FDA
recommend all US dogs and cats stay on preventives year-round, with annual
testing even for dogs on prevention. Heartworm has been diagnosed in all 50
states; the 2024 AHS guidelines added routine screening for cats.
— American Heartworm Society,
https://www.heartwormsociety.org/pet-owner-resources/heartworm-basics

## Bites and children

**Most bites to children come from a familiar dog.** 77% of biting dogs belong
to the victim's family or a friend. 89.8% of dogs involved in facial bites to
children were known to the child. Children 5 and under are at high risk of
facial bites from a familiar dog and are more likely than older children to
need hospitalisation. Children under 12 account for close to half of
dog-bite-related emergency department visits.
— AVMA, *Preventing dog bites*,
https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/pet-owners/dog-bite-prevention ;
AVMA press materials,
https://www.avma.org/news/press-releases/national-dog-bite-prevention-week-april-12-18-avma-emphasizes-protecting

**Breed is a poor predictor of bite risk** and the AVMA has published a
background paper saying so.
— AVMA, *The Role of Breed in Dog Bite Risk and Prevention*,
https://www.avma.org/sites/default/files/resources/dog_bite_risk_and_prevention_bgnd.pdf

**AVMA guidance:** never leave a child unsupervised with a dog, including the
family pet; pet on the back or shoulders rather than the face, ears or tail.
— AVMA, as above.

## Training method

**AVSAB's position is unambiguous:** based on current evidence, only
reward-based methods should be used for all dog training including behaviour
problems, and aversive methods — electronic collars, prong collars, choke
chains, leash corrections, and other physical or psychological punishment —
should not be used. The stated risks of aversive methods are fear, anxiety,
stress, aggression, stress-related illness, pessimism and a worse relationship
with the owner.
— AVSAB, *Humane Dog Training Position Statement*,
https://avsab.org/resources/position-statements/ ; AVMA coverage,
https://www.avma.org/javma-news/2021-11-01/veterinary-behaviorists-no-role-aversive-dog-training-practices

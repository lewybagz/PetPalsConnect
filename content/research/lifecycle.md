# Verified claims: the life course

Puppy and kitten, adolescence, the long middle, decline, and the end. This file
crosses species because the questions do: what is normal for this age, what is
a problem, and what is the thing nobody warned me about.

---

## Puppies: the first sixteen weeks

See `dogs.md` for the AVSAB position statement, the socialisation checklist and
house-training. The three claims that anchor everything else:

- **The primary window is roughly 3 to 14–16 weeks**, when sociability
  outweighs fear.
- **Socialisation should begin before the vaccine series finishes** — AVSAB
  puts classes as early as 7–8 weeks, after one set of vaccines, in a healthy
  puppy.
- **More deaths in dogs under three are attributable to behavioural problems
  than to infectious disease.**

— AVSAB, *Position Statement on Puppy Socialization*,
https://avsab.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Puppy_Socialization_Position_Statement_Download_-_10-3-14.pdf

## Kittens: an even earlier, even shorter window

**The feline sensitive period is roughly 2–7 weeks**, per Karsh & Turner
(1988), extending to around 9 weeks in McCune (1995). It closes before most
kittens go to their new homes, which is the whole problem.
— reviewed in *Socialising kitties: A quantitative survey of US cat owner
attitudes towards kitten and adult cat socialisation programmes*,
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12277100/ ;
Companion Animal Psychology, *The Sensitive Period for Socialization in Puppies
and Kittens*,
https://www.companionanimalpsychology.com/2017/07/the-sensitive-period-for-socialization.html

**Handling dose matters, and it was measured.** Kittens handled **40 minutes a
day between 3 and 7 weeks** spent more time in close contact with people at one
year old than kittens handled 15 minutes a day. Handling of **5–15 minutes a
day** in early life is associated with reduced latency to approach unfamiliar
people.
— Karsh & Turner; McCune, as reviewed above.

**Additional socialisation in rescue settings works.** Kittens given extra
socialisation — handling by multiple people, exposure to household sounds —
showed less fearful behaviour toward humans, and their caretakers reported
stronger attachment at one year.
— Casey & Bradshaw (2008), *The effects of additional socialisation for kittens
in a rescue centre on their behaviour and suitability as a pet*, Applied Animal
Behaviour Science,
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168159108000051

> **Editorial note.** The practical consequence is uncomfortable and worth
> stating: for a kitten adopted at 10 or 12 weeks, the window has largely
> closed, and what remains is careful, low-pressure work rather than the
> catch-up most owners assume is possible. That is an argument for asking a
> breeder or rescue what handling the kitten had, and it is one of the few
> genuinely actionable things a prospective owner can ask.

## Adolescence

See `dogs.md`. The core finding: Asher et al. (2020) found reduced
responsiveness at eight months **specific to the caregiver**, not to strangers,
more pronounced in dogs with less secure attachment — coinciding with the peak
age of relinquishment.

## Cognitive dysfunction

**Prevalence rises steeply with age.** Surveys suggest **14% to 22.5% of dogs
over eight** show signs of cognitive impairment. By owner report, **28% of dogs
aged 11–12** show at least one sign, rising to **68% of dogs aged 15–16**.
— reviewed in *The Science of Canine Cognitive Decline and Dysfunction*, WSAVA
2019 Congress proceedings,
https://www.vin.com/apputil/content/defaultadv1.aspx?pId=24437&id=9382955 ;
AKC Canine Health Foundation resources,
https://www.akcchf.org/agingdogdementia/veterinary/

**It is drastically underdiagnosed.** In one study identifying CDS prevalence
of 14.2% in dogs over eight using a validated questionnaire, **only 13% had
been diagnosed**. Other work reports far lower diagnosis rates still. The
common reason given is that owners attribute the changes to normal ageing.
— as above; AVMA, *New diagnostic tools for identifying early stages of canine
dementia*,
https://www.avma.org/news/new-diagnostic-tools-identifying-early-stages-canine-dementia

**DISHAA** is the screening framework used in practice: **D**isorientation,
**I**nteraction changes, **S**leep-wake cycle changes, **H**ouse-soiling,
**A**ctivity changes, **A**nxiety.
— Purina Institute, *Cognitive Dysfunction Syndrome in dogs*,
https://www.purinainstitute.com/science-of-nutrition/advancing-brain-health/cognitive-dysfunction-syndrome

**Screen medically at the same time.** In a trial of 100 senior dogs flagged by
DISHAA screening, 15 had markedly abnormal laboratory findings — cognitive
signs and medical disease overlap heavily in old animals.
— reviewed at https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7843503/

## Quality of life

**The HHHHHMM scale**, published by Alice Villalobos, scores seven parameters
from 1 to 10: **H**urt, **H**unger, **H**ydration, **H**ygiene, **H**appiness,
**M**obility, and **M**ore good days than bad. A score above 5 in each
category, or a total above 35, is described as an acceptable quality of life
for continuing end-of-life care.
— Villalobos, *Quality of Life Scale*,
https://www.pethospicevet.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/QualityofLifeScale.pdf ;
Veterinary Partner, *Assessing Quality of Life & Euthanasia in Companion
Animals*,
https://veterinarypartner.vin.com/default.aspx?pid=19239&id=4951966

**It is a tracking tool as much as a decision tool** — its stated purpose
includes measuring whether a palliative plan is working and tuning it, not only
deciding when to stop.
— Villalobos, as above.

**It has been examined empirically**, including a validation study in an
Italian context that also explored owners' grief.
— https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10044252/

> **Editorial note.** Present the scale as scaffolding for a conversation with
> a vet, never as an arithmetic verdict. The number's real function is to make
> a diffuse dread into seven specific observable things, and to let somebody
> notice a trend they were too close to see. Say that explicitly, and say that
> the decision belongs to the person and their vet.

## Grief

**Pet loss is a recognised case of disenfranchised grief** — grief that is not
or cannot be openly acknowledged, publicly mourned, or socially supported.
Grief after companion animal loss has been described as comparable in intensity
to grief after human loss, while being routinely stigmatised, minimised or
dismissed.
— *Disenfranchised Grief and Meaning Reconstruction in the Wake of Animal
Loss*, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41440243/ ;
*Coping with the Loss of a Beloved Pet and Disenfranchised Grief*, Springer,
https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-031-38971-9_2062-1

**The consequence of disenfranchisement is suppression.** Where people sense
their grief is not considered worthy of acknowledgement, they inhibit the
grieving response or hide it — which is the mechanism by which unsupported
grief becomes prolonged.
— as above; Association for Pet Loss and Bereavement,
https://www.aplb.org/overcoming-the-social-stigma-of-losing-a-pet-considerations-for-counseling-professionals/

**It affects veterinary staff too.** In one study, 25% of veterinarians did not
feel supported, 30% felt misunderstood, and 30% were unable to express their
grief naturally.
— *Companion animal bereavement in veterinary professionals*,
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0034528826002286

**Support exists and is mostly free.** Several veterinary schools run pet-loss
support lines, and the ASPCA operates a pet loss hotline.
— as above.

> **Editorial note.** This article should not offer stages, timelines, or
> reassurance that it gets easier by a particular week. It should name the
> disenfranchisement, say the intensity is documented rather than excessive,
> list the support that exists, and stop. Anything more is the article
> performing comfort at the reader.
